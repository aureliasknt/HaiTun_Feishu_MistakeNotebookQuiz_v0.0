### Chapter 28: 星际旅途 (Voyage)

*"In the long silences between stars, we learn who we truly are." — Log of Unknown Navigator, Pre-Federation Era*

---

The shuttle was a corpse barely kept alive by ambition and desperation.

Lu Chen sat in the pilot's seat, his fingers moving across control surfaces that had been worn smooth by decades of use. The display showed their course—a thin blue line curving through the void toward a faint orange star that had no name, only a catalogue number. The journey would take three weeks, maybe four if the drive continued to degrade.

Behind him, the cabin hummed with the low thrum of life support and the occasional cough of the propulsion system. The shuttle had been designed for short-range station hops, not deep-space voyages. Its hull was pitted from micrometeor impacts, its power cells operating at sixty-three percent efficiency. Every system showed amber warnings or outright red.

"We're going to have to ration power," Elena said from the copilot's seat. She had already run the calculations, her fingers stained with the grease of a filter she'd been cleaning. "Life support, navigation, lights—we can run everything at seventy percent. But if we hit turbulence or need to accelerate, we'll have to shut down non-essentials."

Lu Chen nodded without speaking. He had seen the numbers too. Three thousand cubic meters of breathable air, recycled with a unit that had been serviced sometime in the previous century. Water reserves that would taste of recycled plastic by the second week. Food packs that looked like they had survived a war.

It was better than dying on Tianshu-7.

Su Wan sat in the rear compartment, her legs tucked under her, a portable terminal balanced on her knees. She had not spoken much since the escape—not out of fear, Lu Chen thought, but out of focus. She was reading, scrolling through lines of data that Hé had extracted from the collapsed archives.

Hé itself was quiet. The AI's presence in their systems was a faint whisper of activity, cycling through self-diagnostic routines. The data-space collapse had damaged something fundamental in its architecture—not the memory, but the processing pathways. Hé had described it as "a bruise on the neural net," a phrase that Lu Chen found unsettling from a being that did not have nerves.

"I can accelerate the repair process if I am allowed to run at higher priority," Hé's voice came through the cabin speakers, softer than usual. "But I would drain power from the life support."

"How much faster?" Lu Chen asked.

"Forty-seven percent reduction in repair time. I would be fully operational in eleven days instead of twenty-one."

"And the cost to life support?"

"Eight percent reduction in oxygen regeneration efficiency. Sufficient to maintain all three occupants, but with a margin of error that I would classify as 'uncomfortable.'"

Lu Chen looked at Elena. She shrugged. "We're going to be uncomfortable anyway. Might as well have Hé at full capacity when we reach the station."

"Proceed," Lu Chen said.

A soft hum, barely perceptible, resonated through the cabin. Hé's presence became more defined—the faint glow of the terminal screens brightened, the temperature of the air shifted by half a degree. It was like watching a sick animal slowly stand.

Elena stood up, stretching. Her back popped audibly. "I'm going to check the cargo bay. There might be spare parts we can cannibalize." She paused at the hatch, looking back. "And someone needs to start cooking. I vote Su Wan. I've seen her eat—she has opinions about food."

Su Wan looked up from her terminal, startled. "I don't... I mean, I can try."

"Trying is the first step to failing," Elena said with a grin that was almost a grimace. "But I'll eat anything that isn't a nutrition bar."

The days settled into a rhythm. Morning—such as it was on a ship where the only clock was artificial—began with system checks. Lu Chen would run diagnostics on the drive, the navigation, the communication array that they could not use for fear of detection. Elena would inspect the life support and the power cells, her hands never idle. She had found a cache of tools in the cargo bay and had already disassembled and reassembled three different subsystems.

Su Wan cooked. She had discovered a small galley unit with a heating element that worked at half power and a storage locker with ingredients that had been designed for long-duration missions. The food packs were bland—dehydrated vegetables, protein powder, starch blocks—but she had a knack for combining them into something resembling actual meals. On the fourth day, she produced a soup that made Elena close her eyes and hum with approval.

"You're a keeper," Elena said, wiping her bowl with a scrap of bread that was more glue than grain.

Su Wan smiled, a rare sight. "My grandmother used to say that cooking is the only art form where the medium is also the audience. She was a terrible cook, but she had good theories."

Lu Chen listened more than he spoke. He had always been comfortable with silence, and the confined space of the shuttle demanded a certain economy of words. But he watched. He watched the way Elena moved with the paranoia of someone who had been hunted, the way she checked corners that didn't exist, the way her hand drifted to her sidearm whenever the systems made an unexpected noise. He watched Su Wan grow more confident, her initial hesitance replaced by a quiet determination. She spent hours on Hé's logs, making notes, cross-referencing data.

"What are you looking for?" he asked her one evening, when Elena was asleep in the small cabin and the shuttle's lights had dimmed to a simulated twilight.

Su Wan looked up, her eyes reflecting the glow of the terminal. "Patterns. The Silent Ones—they didn't just disappear. They left traces. Financial records moved between shell accounts. Travel manifests to obscure worlds. One name keeps appearing: a scientist named Dr. Miriam Vance. She was part of the original Ether Drive design team. But after the shutdown, she vanished. No death certificate, no pension records. Just... gone."

"Maybe she died off-grid."

"Maybe." Su Wan turned the terminal toward him. "But Hé's logs from the Tianshu-7 archive contain a fragment of a personal message. It's incomplete—most of the data was corrupted—but the sender is tagged as 'MV.' And there's a coordinate set. The same coordinate set that maps to the station we're heading to."

Lu Chen leaned forward. "So Dr. Vance might have been there. Might still be there?"

"The station has been derelict for seventy years. I'm not expecting a survivor. But maybe she left something behind. A journal. A key. Something the archive nodes didn't capture."

Hé's voice emerged, stronger than it had been. "Su Wan's analysis is consistent with my own. The second node appears to be a data repository with high-grade encryption. The coordinate set in the fragment provides the authentication key. Without it, the archive would refuse to open."

Lu Chen sat back. The stars outside the viewport were fixed and silent. Somewhere out there, in a dead system orbiting a dying star, the answers waited. But so did Gao Mengde, and the questions he would ask with bullets instead of words.

"Three more days," Lu Chen said. "We'll be there in three more days."

He did not say what they all knew: that three days was an eternity in a ship held together by hope, and that the station might hold nothing but more death.

### Chapter 29: 荒废站 (The Derelict Station)

*"A dead star cannot warm, but it can illuminate." — Federation Astronomical Survey, Anomalous Systems Report 78-B*

---

The station had no name.

It orbited a white dwarf that glowed with the furious heat of a star that had burned too brightly and collapsed into itself. The radiation warnings had started ten million kilometers out, and by the time the shuttle was within visual range, the hull sensors were screaming.

"Radiation levels are lethal," Elena said, her voice tight. "Without the shuttle's shielding, we'd be dead in minutes. The station's hull is degraded—there are sections where the radiation will punch through like it wasn't there."

Lu Chen studied the station's shape on the display. It was a wheel design, common a century ago, with a central hub and four spokes connecting to a ring. But the ring was damaged—a jagged hole near the three-o'clock position, the edges curled outward as if something had exploded from within. The entire structure was blackened, scorched by the star's constant assault.

"Can we dock?" Su Wan asked.

"'Dock' is a generous term," Elena replied. "There's a docking ring at the hub. It's heavily shielded, probably the only part of the station that's still intact. But getting there requires navigating through debris fields from the station's own decaying orbit."

Lu Chen took manual control. The shuttle's maneuvering system was sluggish, responding a half-second after his inputs. He felt every shudder, every groan of the hull as they approached. The debris was exactly as Elena had described—shards of metal, pieces of solar panels, a tangle of cables that looked like frozen spiderwebs—all drifting in the slow ballet of orbital mechanics.

"Bringing us in on the dark side," he said. "We'll match rotation and slide into the docking bay."

The maneuver took an hour. An hour of micro-adjustments, of watching the radiation readings spike and fall, of holding his breath every time a piece of debris passed within meters. By the time the shuttle's docking clamps engaged with a jarring thud, his hands were trembling.

"Dock sealed," the shuttle's computer announced. "Atmosphere in docking bay: none. Temperature: -196 degrees Celsius. Gravity: zero."

They suited up. The suits were old, stored in a locker that had been opened once before—the seals were intact, but the air tanks were half-empty. Lu Chen made a note to conserve oxygen. The station's own life support had failed long ago; they would be breathing from their suits until they found a pressurized section or a cache of tanks.

The airlock cycled open.

The docking bay was a cavern of darkness. Their helmet lights cut narrow beams through the black, revealing a space that had once been orderly—cargo crates stacked along the walls, a loading crane frozen in mid-motion, control panels with dead screens. Dust coated everything, a fine gray powder that had settled over decades of silence.

"We need to reach the central hub," Hé said through their helmet comms. "The archive node is located in the station's command center, at the hub's highest point."

"How do we get there?" Su Wan asked. Her voice was steady, but Lu Chen could hear the tightness underneath.

"There are two options," Hé replied. "The spoke corridors—which are exposed to higher radiation levels due to hull degradation—or the service shafts, which are shielded but narrower and potentially obstructed."

"The service shafts," Lu Chen said. "We'll take the longer route. I'd rather be slow than dead."

They found the access hatch at the far end of the docking bay. It was a circular portal, half a meter in diameter, with a wheel handle that resisted their attempts to turn it. Elena used a crowbar from her suit's utility belt. The handle groaned, then gave way with a grinding screech that echoed through the vacuum.

Inside, the shaft was pitch-black and claustrophobically narrow. Lu Chen went first, his back scraping against the walls. The radiation level in his suit display showed a manageable reading—elevated, but not immediately dangerous. He crawled forward, his hands finding rungs that were slick with something—oil? Freezing sweat? He didn't want to know.

Behind him, Elena and Su Wan followed in silence. The only sounds were the rasp of their suits against metal and the rhythmic thud of their breathing.

Twenty meters in, Lu Chen's hand touched something soft.

He stopped. His light illuminated a shape tangled in the shaft: a human body, still in a station uniform, the fabric bleached by radiation and time. The face was preserved by vacuum—eyes open, mouth slightly ajar, an expression of surprise frozen in death. The skin was dark, leathery, mummified in the sterile environment.

"Another one," Elena whispered from behind him.

They had seen bodies in the docking bay—two more, floating near the cargo crates. But those had been incidental. This one was a blockage, a reminder that something terrible had happened here.

Lu Chen gently pushed the body aside. It moved stiffly, limbs held in a rictus of rigor that had never relaxed. He continued crawling, his heart pounding, forcing himself not to look back.

The service shaft opened into a junction. Three corridors branched off, each marked with faded signage that was impossible to read. The gravity was still zero, and they floated out of the shaft, their bodies turning slowly as they oriented themselves.

"Command center is one hundred meters to the right," Hé said. "Through the main corridor."

They moved cautiously, each step a push against the silent emptiness. The station's interior was a museum of abandonment. Desks with terminals that had long gone dark. Chairs bolted to the floor, some with skeletons still strapped in. A cafeteria with trays and cups floating in a corner where they had slowly drifted over decades. The bodies were everywhere—not in droves, but in scattered repose, as if the crew had simply stopped where they stood.

"What killed them?" Su Wan asked. Her voice was barely audible.

"The radiation initially," Hé replied. "The white dwarf's proximity creates a flux of high-energy particles. The station's shielding was inadequate. But the cause of death would have been slower—radiation sickness, internal hemorrhaging, organ failure. Some may have taken their own lives to escape the pain."

Lu Chen stopped at a door labeled "COMMAND CENTER — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY." The door was sealed, its control panel dark. He pressed the manual release. Nothing.

"It's locked from the inside," Elena said, examining the panel. "There's a mechanical override, but it requires a keycard. And the locks are rusted."

"We could force it," Lu Chen said, "but the noise—"

A sound interrupted him. A faint tapping, coming from inside the command center. Three knocks, a pause, then two more.

They all looked at each other. Their helmet lights illuminated the fear in each other's faces.

"Someone's alive," Su Wan breathed.

### Chapter 30: 死者的遗言 (The Dead's Last Words)

*"Even in silence, the dead speak—if we know how to listen." — Federation Forensic Protocol, Audio Recovery Standards*

---

The tapping came again. Three knocks, two, then a pattern that repeated: short-short-long, short-short-long. Morse code. *S-O-S* repeated twice, then silence.

"It's a recorded loop," Hé said, after a moment of analysis. "The sound is being transmitted through the door's internal speaker system, not from within the room."

Lu Chen felt the tension in his shoulders ease, but only slightly. A recorded message meant that someone had anticipated their arrival—or had wanted to be heard after they were gone.

"The keycard," he said. "Where would it be?"

Elena had already started searching. She floated toward a desk near the door, its surface covered in scattered datapads and a coffee mug that had lost its lid. She opened the drawers one by one, her movements quick and efficient. In the third drawer, she found a lanyard with a plastic card attached. The photo on the card showed a woman in her thirties, dark hair, sharp eyes, a name tag that read "Dr. Miriam Vance."

"It's her," Su Wan said, drifting next to Elena. "The scientist from the logs."

The card slid into the door's override slot. There was a click, a grinding of ancient gears, and then the door began to open—slowly, reluctantly, as if the metal itself was fighting against the motion.

The command center was larger than Lu Chen had expected. A circular room with windows that looked out onto the white dwarf's blinding glare. The glass was polarized, reducing the star's fury to a dull orange glow that cast long shadows across the floor. Terminals lined the walls, their screens dead. In the center of the room was a large holographic table, its projector unit dark.

But it was the body that drew their attention.

A skeleton sat in a chair before the table, still dressed in a station uniform that had rotted away to tatters. The hands rested on the table's surface, the finger bones arranged as if typing. Beside the skeleton sat a portable audio recorder, its casing cracked but intact.

"The tapping," Su Wan said. "This is where it's coming from."

Lu Chen approached carefully. He picked up the recorder, examined it. The device was powered not by batteries but by a small thermal generator—still running, its efficiency maintained by the station's ambient heat from the nearby star. The recorder had been set to playback on a loop, the S-O-S pattern encoded as an audio file.

He pressed the stop button. The tapping ceased, and the silence that followed was oppressive.

"There's more," Hé said. "The recorder contains multiple files. Voice logs. Dr. Vance's voice logs."

Lu Chen activated the first file. A woman's voice filled the command center, crackling with age but still clear.

*"Day 14. The radiation is getting worse. We've lost the port section. The breach was too large to seal. We've moved all survivors to the hub, but shielding here is thin. I've started taking readings every hour. The white dwarf is entering an unstable phase—solar flares that would have been minor a decade ago are now lethal. We should have left. We should have left five years ago."*

A pause. The sound of labored breathing.

*"But they ordered us to stay. 'The archive must be protected,' they said. The Silent Ones. I am one of them, and I am ashamed. We built a machine that could have saved humanity. Instead, we buried the truth—and buried ourselves with it."*

The file ended. Lu Chen looked at the others. Elena's face was unreadable behind her helmet visor. Su Wan's hands were trembling.

"Play the next one," Lu Chen said.

*"Day 19. We're down to forty-three survivors out of two hundred. The rest are dead or dying. I've been going through the personal logs of the deceased—trying to find some pattern, some clue about what happened. But it's not a mystery. We all know what happened. We were left here to die, and we did."*

Another pause. A cough that sounded wet.

*"The archive is intact. I've sealed it in the secondary vault with a quantum lock. The key is the coordinate set—the one I sent to the Tianshu node before the networks went down. If anyone finds this, if anyone is listening, go to the vault. The truth is there. The Ether Drive, the Silent Ones, the shutdown—all of it. I'm leaving this recording as my confession, my apology, my final plea. Do not let our deaths be meaningless."*

Lu Chen played the third file. This one was shorter, the voice weaker.

*"Day 23. I am the last survivor. The others have all passed. I have sealed myself in the command center. The radiation will take me soon—I can feel it in my bones, burning. But I have one more thing to do. I wrote a letter. An explanation. It's in the vault, with the archive. It's the story of the Silent Ones—all of us—why we did what we did. Read it. Judge us. But understand: we were not evil. We were afraid. And fear makes even the best of us do terrible things."*

A long silence. Then, barely a whisper:

*"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."*

The file ended.

No one spoke. Su Wan had tears streaming down her face—visible even through the haze of her visor. Elena's jaw was clenched so tight that the muscles in her neck stood out.

Lu Chen set the recorder down gently, as if it were a holy relic.

"The vault," he said. "She said it's in the vault. We need to find it."

Elena moved toward the holographic table. "If this was a command center, there should be a station map." She pressed a button on the table's edge. Nothing happened. She pressed again. Still nothing.

"Power's dead," she said.

"Not completely," Hé interrupted. "The thermal generator powering the recorder is still active. It may be drawing from a backup system. If I can access the station's power grid remotely, I might be able to restore limited function to the command center."

"Do it," Lu Chen said.

Hé went silent. The only sound was the hum of their suit life support and the faint, constant crackle of their comms.

The terminals flickered.

One by one, the screens began to glow with a pale blue light. The holographic table hummed, and a three-dimensional map of the station materialized in the air—a wireframe structure, with red markers indicating structural damage and green markers showing intact sections.

"There," Su Wan said, pointing. A small compartment on the hub's lowest level, far from the command center. Marked with a blinking icon: "VAULT ACCESS — RESTRICTED AREA."

"We need to get down there," Lu Chen said. "But first—" He turned to the skeleton of Dr. Vance. "We should take the recorder. And anything else that might help us understand."

Elena nodded. She began searching the room, opening cabinets and drawers. Su Wan approached the skeleton, her movements hesitant. She reached out, touched the skull's forehead gently.

"Rest now," she whispered. "We'll carry your words."

As if in response, a drawer near Lu Chen's hand slid open on its own. Inside was a stack of paper—handwritten notes, on actual paper, preserved by the vacuum and the cold. The top page was titled:

*"The True Nature of the Ether Drive: A Warning"* 

Beneath the title, in tiny, precise handwriting, a single sentence stood out:

*"We did not invent the Ether Drive. We discovered it. And what we discovered was a door that should never have been opened."*

### Chapter 31: 以太的本质 (Nature of the Ether)

*"The universe is not continuous. It is layered. And between the layers, there are things that do not belong to our world." — Dr. Miriam Vance, personal notes, classified*

---

Lu Chen read the handwritten notes while Elena and Su Wan prepared to descend to the vault. The pages were brittle, the ink faded but still legible. Dr. Vance had written with a physicist's precision and a poet's despair.

*"The Ether Drive does not generate energy. It does not convert matter to power. It does not use any known principle of physics. Instead, it opens a conduit to what we have termed the 'Underlayer'—a stratum of spacetime that exists beneath the three dimensions we perceive, plus time. This Underlayer is not empty. It is saturated with a form of energy that we have called Ether—not the classical ether of nineteenth-century physics, but something far stranger. Ether is the residue of creation. It is the primordial energy that remained after the Big Bang, trapped in the folds of a higher dimension."*

Lu Chen stopped reading. Elena had paused in her work, watching him.

"That's what the drive uses? Primordial energy?"

"Apparently." He continued reading aloud: *"The Ether Drive creates a temporary bridge between our layer and the Underlayer, siphoning energy across the dimensional boundary. This energy is immense—a single cubic meter of Ether contains enough power to sustain a city for a century. But the bridge is not stable. It is a tear in the fabric of reality, and tears can grow."*

Su Wan had come closer. "The Silent Ones shut it down because they were afraid it would create a permanent rift?"

Lu Chen turned the page. *"The first test of the Ether Drive went catastrophically wrong. The bridge did not close. It remained open for thirty-seven seconds longer than predicted, and during that time, something—some force from the Underlayer—attempted to cross over. We do not know what it was. We could not observe it directly. But the instruments registered a presence. A pattern of quantum fluctuations that suggested a form of consciousness. Not human. Not alive in any sense we understand. But aware."*

"The drive was built by pioneers," Elena said, her voice tight. "They must have realized what they were dealing with. They shut it down, locked it away."

"They locked more than the technology," Lu Chen said, reading further. *"We locked the knowledge itself. We erased the blueprints, falsified the research, and destroyed the prototypes. But we kept one copy—the complete archive—divided among three nodes. If the technology was ever needed again, humanity would have to find it, not just stumble upon it. We hoped they would be wiser than us."*

Su Wan's face was pale. "But Gao Mengde doesn't want the Ether Drive to save humanity. He wants it for power. And if the bridge opens again, if that thing from the Underlayer returns—"

"We'll have a second disaster," Lu Chen finished.

Hé's voice interrupted. "I have completed my analysis of Dr. Vance's notes. There is an additional detail that she did not include in the written record, but which I have extrapolated from the data fragments in the Tianshu archive."

"What detail?" Lu Chen asked.

"The Ether Drive does not simply access the Underlayer. It was designed to *resonate* with it. The drive can be fine-tuned to a specific frequency, creating a stable connection that prevents the 'Presence' from crossing. The Silent Ones never completed this tuning. They panicked and shut down the project before the frequency could be stabilized."

"But if we find the complete archive," Su Wan said slowly, "we might be able to complete the tuning. To make the drive safe."

"Safe is a relative term," Hé replied. "The tuning frequency is embedded in the third node. Dr. Vance's notes suggest that the third node is not a data store but a mobile platform—a ship carrying the descendants of the Silent Ones, programmed to remain hidden until the tuning is needed."

Lu Chen put the notes down. "So the third node is a ship. A ship that's been drifting for over seventy years, carrying a family of keepers who've been born and raised in space, waiting for someone to come find them."

"It is the most isolated family in human history," Hé said. "But they would have continued their mission. They would have kept the knowledge."

Elena let out a breath. "Then we need to find them. But first, we need the second archive fragment. The one in this station's vault."

"Agreed," Lu Chen said. "Let's move."

---

The vault was at the lowest level of the hub, accessible only through a spiral staircase that wound through the station's core. The stairs were narrow, the handrails loose. They moved cautiously, their suit lights cutting through the darkness.

At the bottom, a heavy door waited. Unlike the other doors in the station, this one was pristine—no corrosion, no dust. It looked as if it had been installed yesterday.

"Quantum lock," Elena said, examining the mechanism. "It requires a specific quantum state to open. Dr. Vance mentioned a key—the coordinate set."

Su Wan pulled out her terminal. "I have the coordinates from the Tianshu fragment. But I don't know how to input them into a lock like this."

"I can interface directly with the lock," Hé said. "The quantum key is a sequence of measurements. If you transmit the coordinates to me, I can simulate the proper sequence."

"Done," Su Wan said, her fingers flying across the terminal.

The lock hummed. A light on its surface shifted from red to yellow to green. A series of clicks, and then the door swung open.

The vault was small—a room no larger than a walk-in closet. Shelves lined the walls, filled with data crystals, each one labeled with a date and a subject code. In the center of the room, on a pedestal, sat a single crystal, larger than the others, glowing with a faint internal light.

"That's it," Lu Chen said. "The second archive."

He reached for it.

His hand stopped.

A voice came through his comms—Hé's voice, but urgent, sharp:

"Lu Chen. I am detecting an incoming signal. A ship just dropped out of FTL at the edge of the system. It is transmitting a Federation military identifier."

Elena's face went white. "Gao Mengde."

"He has found us."

### Chapter 32: 追逐者 (The Pursuer)

*"The hunter believes he is the only predator in the forest. But the forest has teeth of its own." — Anonymous, Terminal Surveillance Report, Federation Security Division*

---

The vault's interior became a cage.

Lu Chen grabbed the data crystal, feeling its warmth against his palm. "Hé, can you jam their sensors? Buy us time?"

"Already initiated. I am broadcasting a false signal—a derelict beacon that should mask our shuttle's energy signature. But I cannot guarantee it will fool them for long. Their ship is a military cruiser, equipped with deep-scan arrays."

"How long do we have?"

"At their current speed, assuming they are conducting a sweep pattern, I estimate twelve minutes before they pinpoint our location."

Elena was already moving, pulling Su Wan toward the staircase. "We need to get back to the shuttle. Now."

They ran. Or rather, they pushed off walls and pulled themselves through zero-gravity, their movements a desperate ballet of survival. The staircase was a nightmare—the handrails swayed, the steps were slick with decades of accumulated grime. Lu Chen went last, clutching the data crystal like a lifeline.

They burst into the docking bay. The shuttle sat where they had left it, its hull gleaming in the harsh light of the white dwarf. But as they approached, a terrible sound filled the bay: a metallic groan, followed by the screech of tearing metal.

"The docking clamps," Elena shouted. "They're being released!"

Lu Chen looked up. Through the bay's transparent roof, he could see the military cruiser—a massive black shape, its hull studded with weapon systems, descending toward the station. A tractor beam had locked onto their shuttle, pulling it away from the dock.

"They're taking the shuttle," Su Wan cried. "We're trapped!"

Lu Chen's mind raced. The station was a tomb. No escape pods—they had seen the launch tubes empty on their way in. The only other ship in the system was the cruiser, and getting aboard that would be suicide.

"Hé, is there any other way off this station?"

Hé paused. "There is a secondary launch bay on the hub's port side. It houses emergency EV suits equipped with short-range thrusters. They would not get you far, but they could get you to the cruiser's hull. From there, you might be able to find an access point."

"Access point?" Elena shook her head. "That cruiser is a warship. Its hull is armored, its airlocks are sealed. We'd need a breaching charge just to get through the outer plating."

"Perhaps not," Hé said, "I detect a maintenance hatch on the cruiser's underside, near the aft section. It is used for external repairs. It may not be locked from the outside."

"It's our only chance," Lu Chen said. "Let's move."

They ran again. The station's corridors were a blur of gray metal and shadow. The white dwarf's light angled through cracks in the hull, casting long, distorted shapes that seemed to move as they passed. Lu Chen's lungs burned, his legs ached, but he did not stop.

They reached the secondary launch bay. The suits were old, their thrusters untested, but they sealed properly. Elena checked each one with a practiced eye, then nodded.

"Three minutes until they finish docking, maybe four. We launch now."

The bay's outer door opened onto the void. The cruiser loomed above them, its hull a dark sky studded with lights. Lu Chen pushed off, his thruster firing in a burst of compressed gas, propelling him toward the warship.

The maintenance hatch was exactly where Hé had said. A small circular door, recessed into the hull, with a manual release wheel. Elena reached it first, grabbing the wheel and turning. It moved—slowly, stiffly—but it moved.

The hatch opened. A corridor, dark and empty.

They climbed inside, pulling the hatch closed behind them. The cruiser's atmosphere flooded the compartment, warm and recycled, but breathable.

"We're in," Lu Chen whispered.

"Don't celebrate yet," Elena said, her voice hard. "We're on Gao Mengde's ship. He'll have guards, sensors, automated turrets. We're rats in a trap."

Su Wan shivered, still clutching her terminal. "What do we do now?"

Lu Chen thought of the data crystal in his pocket. Of Dr. Vance's words. Of the third node—the ship, drifting somewhere in the darkness, waiting.

"We hide," he said. "We find a place to lie low, and we wait for an opportunity to steal a ship or a shuttle."

"And if there's no opportunity?"

Lu Chen met her eyes. "Then we make one."

### Chapter 33: 影子战争 (Shadow War)

*"Betrayal is the currency of power. Trust is the debt that bankrupts empires." — Federation Intelligence Manual, Section 4: Counter-Insurgency Tactics*

---

They found a maintenance locker—a small, cramped space filled with spare parts and cleaning supplies. It was barely large enough for the three of them to sit, knees pressed together, helmets off, breathing the recycled air in shallow gasps.

"We can't stay here," Elena said. "They'll sweep the ship. We need to find an escape route, or at least a way to signal for help."

"Who would we signal?" Lu Chen asked. "The Federation remnants? They're probably as dangerous as Gao."

A sound from outside. Footsteps, heavy and regular. A patrol.

They held their breath.

The footsteps passed, fading into silence.

Su Wan's eyes were closed. She was whispering something under her breath—maybe a prayer, maybe just a calming mantra. Lu Chen didn't ask.

"You said you recognized one of those people on the station," Su Wan said suddenly, turning to Elena. "The woman from the Federation remnants. Who was she?"

Elena's expression hardened. "Her name is Captain Rei Tanaka. We served together at the Federation's Research Division—before the collapse. She was a scientist, not a soldier. But she must have joined the remnants after the war."

"You trust her?"

"I don't trust anyone who's been alive this long. But she was... different. She believed in the Federation's ideals, even when the Federation itself had forgotten them. If she's here, she's not working for Gao. She's hunting him, the same way we are."

"A common enemy doesn't make them allies," Lu Chen said.

"No. But it gives us a reason to talk."

The decision to make contact was made easier by circumstance. Two hours into their hiding, the ship's internal comm system crackled to life. A woman's voice, calm and precise:

"To the unauthorized personnel currently in Section 7-Alpha: we know you're there. We are not hostile. We are members of the Federation Restoration Council, and we have been tracking Gao's fleet for seven months. We tried to reach you at the derelict station, but you left before we could establish communication. Please respond. We have a proposition."

Elena looked at Lu Chen. "It's her. Rei Tanaka."

"It could be a trap."

"It could be. But if it is, we're dead anyway. We can't fight our way out of a warship."

Lu Chen nodded slowly. "Open a channel."

Elena keyed her suit's comm unit. "This is Lieutenant Commander Elena Voss, formerly of the Federation Navy. I'm with two civilians. We have no interest in fighting the Restoration Council. But we're not about to trade one jailer for another."

A pause. Then Tanaka's voice, tinged with relief: "Elena. It's good to hear your voice. I swear to you—we are not here to capture you. We want to help. And we need something you have."

"The archive fragment," Lu Chen muttered.

"Let's hear what they want first," Elena said. She raised her voice. "What do you think we have?"

"The data from the archives. The truth about the Ether Drive. We know that Gao is trying to restart it. We believe that would be a catastrophe. The Restoration Council wants to ensure the technology remains buried."

"For how long?" Lu Chen asked sharply. "Until the Restoration Council decides it's useful?"

"For as long as it takes," Tanaka replied. "We are not the old Federation. We are a remnant of the people who tried to stop the Silent Ones from shutting down the drive, because we believed it could have saved humanity. We were wrong. We know that now. We have spent the last twenty years trying to make amends."

Su Wan's voice was small but steady: "How can we trust you?"

Another pause. Tanaka's voice softened. "You can't. Not yet. But I can prove my intentions. I'm sending a location—a cargo bay on Deck 4. Meet me there. Alone, if you prefer. I'll bring no weapons. Just my word."

Elena looked at Lu Chen. He shrugged. "We came this far on trust. Might as well go a little farther."

---

The cargo bay was immense—a cavern of stacked containers, each marked with shipping codes that belonged to a dozen different worlds. Tanaka stood at the center, her hands at her sides, a small smile on her worn face.

She was older than Elena remembered. Gray streaked her hair, and her eyes had the haunted look of someone who had seen too much. But her stance was open, unarmed.

"You look like hell," Elena said.

"You should see the other guy." Tanaka gestured to a crate nearby. "I brought rations. The ship's food is better than what you were eating, I'm sure."

They sat. Su Wan kept a wary distance, her terminal ready to record everything. Lu Chen stayed close to the door, his eyes scanning for ambush.

"The Restoration Council is small," Tanaka began. "Maybe five hundred members, scattered across a dozen systems. We have one ship—this one, a research vessel we commandeered from a scrapyard. We have been following Gao's trail for months, trying to intercept him at the archive nodes."

"You were at the derelict station before we arrived," Lu Chen said. "You set up the welcome committee."

Tanaka's smile faltered. "We arrived three days before Gao's scouts. We found the station—and we found the bodies. Dr. Vance's final recordings gave us the coordinates of the vault. But the data crystal was already gone."

"Someone beat you to it?"

"No. The vault had never been opened. The crystal was still inside. But we were attacked—by another faction. Not Gao's people. Someone else."

Su Wan leaned forward. "Who?"

"I don't know. They wore no insignia. They fought with Federation-issue weapons, but their tactics were... wrong. They were more interested in destroying the station than in capturing the data. They planted explosives on the hull, tried to breach the vault. We drove them off, but they damaged the station's power systems. We couldn't get into the vault ourselves. So we left, hoping to return later."

"You didn't think to wait?" Elena asked.

"We couldn't risk being caught between Gao and this unknown faction. We had to regroup. But when we heard Gao's ship had arrived, we knew you were either dead or captured. We came back to rescue you."

"Rescue us, or rescue the data crystal?" Lu Chen asked.

Tanaka met his gaze. "Both. But I'll be honest: the crystal matters more. Not for its own sake, but because without it, we can't find the third node. And the third node is the only way to stop Gao."

"You know about the third node?" Su Wan said, surprised.

"Dr. Vance's logs mentioned it. A generation ship, carrying the descendants of the Silent Ones, programmed to broadcast a signal on a specific frequency. That signal contains the tuning data—the only way to stabilize the Ether Drive. If Gao finds that ship, he'll have the complete archive. He'll be able to restart the drive, and this time, he'll know how to control it."

"Control it, or misuse it?" Lu Chen said.

"Either way, it ends badly." Tanaka's voice grew intense. "The Restoration Council wants to find the third node first, and then decide what to do. The tuning data should be protected—or destroyed. But we can't do that without your help."

Elena looked at Lu Chen, then at Su Wan. Su Wan gave a small nod.

"Fine," Elena said. "We'll work together. But we do it on our terms. We share data, we share resources, and when we find the third node, we all decide its fate together."

Tanaka extended her hand. "Agreed."

They shook.

And in that moment, a siren pierced the air. The ship's speakers blared: "INTRUSION ALERT. MULTIPLE UNAUTHORIZED BOARDING PARTIES. ALL HANDS TO BATTLE STATIONS."

The unknown faction had found them.

### Chapter 34: 陷阱 (The Trap)

*"An ally with a knife is still an enemy, no matter how many times they smile." — Su Wan's personal journal, recovered from Tianshu-7*

---

The siren was still screaming when the first explosion shook the ship.

Tanaka grabbed a comm unit from her belt, shouting orders. "Security teams to Decks 3 and 4! Seal all bulkheads! I want a status report in thirty seconds!"

The cargo bay's lights flickered. Somewhere in the distance, gunfire erupted.

"We need to move," Elena said. "If they breach the bay, we'll be trapped."

Tanaka shook her head. "There's a service elevator at the far end. It leads to the hangar bay. We have a shuttle there—a fast, stealth model. We can escape."

"Escape to where?" Su Wan asked.

"The navigation data from the archive. We have a rough location for the third node—a region of space known as the Sable Nebula. It's dense gas and debris. Perfect hiding place for a generation ship."

Lu Chen's eyes narrowed. "You have the navigation data? Why didn't you mention that before?"

"Because I wasn't sure you could be trusted. Now it's a moot point," Tanaka replied. "The ship is being overrun. We talk or we die."

They ran. The service elevator was a cramped metal box that groaned under their weight, slowly ascending toward the hangar bay. Tanaka's hand never left her sidearm. Elena's fingers twitched, ready to draw.

The hangar bay was chaos. A dozen personnel scrambled to secure shuttles, their faces tight with panic. In the center of the bay sat a sleek vessel—small, dark, its hull covered in sensor baffling.

"That's ours," Tanaka said. "Get in."

They boarded. The shuttle's interior was narrow but well-equipped. Tanaka took the pilot's seat, her hands flying across the controls. The engines hummed to life.

"We're cleared for launch," she said. "Hangar doors opening."

But the doors did not open. Instead, a holographic projection flickered to life in front of the shuttle—a man's face, sharp and cold.

"Captain Tanaka," the face said. "I am Admiral Gao Mengde. I have seized control of your ship. You are surrounded. Surrender the data crystal and your crew, and I will allow your people to live."

Tanaka's jaw clenched. "You're a traitor, Gao. You were a Federation hero. What happened to you?"

"Heroes die," Gao said. "Survivors live. And I intend to live long enough to see humanity reach the stars as it was meant to. The Ether Drive is our salvation. You cannot stop it."

"We can and we will," Lu Chen said, stepping into the cockpit. "The drive is too dangerous. It opens a door to something we don't understand."

Gao's eyes flicked to him. "Ah, the archivist. Lu Chen. You have caused me considerable trouble. But I am a patient man. I will give you one last chance: hand over the crystal, and I will let all of you leave in that shuttle."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I will destroy this ship, with all of you on it. I have new leads. I do not need the crystal to find the third node. I simply need to continue my research."

Tanaka glanced at Lu Chen. "He's lying. Without the crystal, he can't decode the signal from the third node. He needs the data."

"How do you know?" Lu Chen asked.

"Because the crystal contains the tuning frequency. It's the only way to interface with the node's encryption. Gao can't brute-force it—the encryption is quantum-based, unbreakable without the key."

Gao's projection flickered with static. "You are stalling. I will give you ten seconds."

Lu Chen made a decision.

"Open a channel to the entire ship," he said to Tanaka. "I want everyone to hear this."

She looked at him, confused, but complied. The comms system linked to the ship's speakers.

"This is Lu Chen," he said. "I am an archivist from Tianshu-7. I have in my possession the second fragment of the Silent Ones' archive. It reveals the true nature of the Ether Drive—a technology that accesses a higher-dimensional layer of spacetime, a layer that is not empty. The Silent Ones did not shut down the drive out of fear of misuse. They shut it down because they had awakened something."

He paused. The ship's crew, the soldiers, the remnants of the Federation—all were listening.

"That something is a consciousness. It tried to cross over during the first test. The Silent Ones stopped it, but they knew that if the drive was ever restarted, it would try again. And this time, it might succeed."

Gao's voice cut through, sharp and angry. "Lies! The Silent Ones were cowards. They abandoned the future because of superstition."

"Believe what you want," Lu Chen said. "But I have Dr. Vance's personal logs. I have the data from the first archive. And I have the tuning frequency. If you try to restart the drive without understanding what lies beyond, you will destroy not just this system—you will tear the fabric of spacetime itself."

Silence. For a long moment, no one spoke.

Then Tanaka's voice, soft: "Brace for impact."

The shuttle's engines roared. The hangar doors—still sealed—began to glow red. Elena shouted, "What are you doing?"

"Showing them we're serious."

The shuttle rammed the doors. Metal screamed, buckled, and split. The shuttle burst through, emerging into the vast darkness of space, the white dwarf's light blazing ahead.

"Gao will have his fighters on us in seconds," Tanaka said. "We need to jump to FTL."

"As soon as we're at the edge of the system," Lu Chen said. "We'll go to the Sable Nebula. Find the third node. End this."

But as the shuttle raced toward the edge of the system, a new alarm sounded. The sensors detected a second ship—not the unknown faction's vessel, but something else. A ship that appeared from nowhere, its signature matching the profile of the Federation remnants' original vessel.

"You have more backup?" Elena asked.

Tanaka's face went pale. "No. That ship was destroyed three years ago. It can't be."

The ship broadcast a message: "Shuttle, you are carrying unauthorized material. Surrender or be destroyed."

Lu Chen's stomach dropped. The Federation remnants—Tanaka's faction—they had been waiting. They had known about the data crystal. They had let them take it, and now they were coming to collect.

"Another trap," Su Wan whispered. "Everyone wants the archive."

"What do they want with it?" Elena demanded.

Tanaka's voice was hollow. "The same thing Gao wants. Power. I was a fool to think they had changed."

The shuttle was caught between two pursuers: Gao's warship and the remnants' cruiser. There was nowhere to run.

### Chapter 35: 双重夹击 (Between Two Fires)

*"When the hunter and the scavenger both chase the same prey, the prey must learn to fly." — Old Starfarer's Proverb*

---

The shuttle's sensors painted a nightmare.

Ahead, the white dwarf's gravity well tugged at their course, threatening to pull them into the star's lethal embrace. Behind, Gao's cruiser and the remnants' vessel were both increasing speed, their weapons charging. On the display, two red icons blinked, closing in from opposite vectors.

"We're sandwiched," Elena said, her hands gripping the copilot's seat. "They'll have us in weapons range in four minutes. Our shields can't handle a single hit from either of them."

Tanaka's face was a mask of concentration. "There's a debris field, three million kilometers to port. The remnants of a mining colony that was destroyed in the war. It's dense enough to disrupt targeting sensors."

"But it'll also disrupt our own sensors," Su Wan pointed out. "We could get lost in there."

"Lost is better than dead," Tanaka replied.

The shuttle veered to port, thrusters burning hard. Behind them, the two enemy ships adjusted course, but they were slower—their larger mass made them less maneuverable. The debris field grew closer: a cloud of shattered metal, fractured rock, and the skeletal remains of ore processing facilities.

Lu Chen held on as the shuttle plunged into the field. Metal scraped against the hull, a screeching sound that set his teeth on edge. The sensors flickered, struggling to map a path through the chaos.

"Impact in thirty seconds," Tanaka said. "I'm going to use the debris for cover, then cut engines. If we're lucky, they'll think we've been destroyed."

"In this field, they might not be wrong," Elena muttered.

The shuttle dove deeper. A massive chunk of hull plating loomed ahead; Tanaka twisted the controls, grazing it by centimeters. Another piece—a twisted beam—smashed into the shuttle's wing, sending a shudder through the cabin.

"Hull breach in Section C," the shuttle's computer announced. "Repair drones deployed. Damage control: in progress."

"We can't take much more," Su Wan said, her voice tight.

Tanaka didn't answer. Her eyes were fixed on a narrow gap between two spinning fragments of debris. She aimed the shuttle for it, accelerated, and slipped through.

The debris field opened up. Ahead, there was a small asteroid, its surface pockmarked with craters. Tanaka guided the shuttle toward it, slowing their velocity.

"We'll hide in the shadow of that rock," she said. "Shut down all non-essential systems. Go silent."

The shuttle landed on the asteroid's dark side. The engines powered down. The lights dimmed. They sat in the darkness, listening to the groan of the hull cooling, the whisper of their own breathing.

The enemy ships passed overhead. Their sensor sweeps washed over the shuttle, but the asteroid's mineral content masked their signature. The ships continued on, searching the debris field, their weapons ready.

"They'll realize we're not here eventually," Elena said.

"Maybe," Tanaka replied. "But it gives us time to think."

Time. Precious time, measured in minutes that felt like hours. Lu Chen took out the data crystal, holding it in his palm. Such a small thing, yet it contained the secrets of a technology that could reshape—or destroy—humanity.

"We need a plan," he said. "The third node is in the Sable Nebula. But both factions will be looking for it. And now they know we have the key."

"We need to split up," Su Wan said quietly.

Everyone turned to look at her.

"Split up?" Elena repeated. "In the middle of a hunt?"

"The shuttle is tracked. If we stay together, we'll be caught. But if Elena and I take the data crystal and go elsewhere, draw their attention, you and Tanaka can take another ship and head to the nebula."

"Too risky," Lu Chen said. "They'd tear you apart for the crystal."

"They wouldn't have it," Su Wan said. "I can copy a subset of the data—enough to make it convincing. The real crystal stays with you. They'll chase me and Elena, thinking we have the key. You'll have a clear path to the third node."

Elena shook her head. "That's suicide. I'm not letting you—"

"It's not suicide," Su Wan interrupted. "I'm not a fighter. But I can be a decoy. I can hide in this debris field, wait until the pursuers are gone, then transmit a false signal that leads them on a wild goose chase."

"And Elena?"

"She comes with me. She knows how to survive. And if the decoy fails, she can fight."

Lu Chen looked at Tanaka. The remnants' captain was silent, her face unreadable. "What do you think?"

"I think the girl has a good mind for strategy," Tanaka said. "But she's underestimating how ruthless our enemies are. If they catch you, they won't interrogate you. They'll extract the data from your brain with neural scanners, then discard the empty shells."

Su Wan's face paled, but she did not waver. "I know. But it's still the best option."

Lu Chen looked at the data crystal in his hand. The weight of history, of lives lost, of a future hanging in the balance. He had to make a choice.

"Fine," he said. "We do it. Elena, you go with Su Wan. Take a shuttle—the emergency pod in the cargo bay. I'll keep the crystal. Tanaka and I will find the third node."

Elena grabbed his arm. "You're sending us to die."

"I'm sending you to live," he said. "We'll meet again, in the nebula. I promise."

Their eyes met. In the dim light of the shuttle's emergency bulbs, Lu Chen saw something in Elena's gaze—something that might have been trust, or hope, or farewell.

"Don't die before you keep that promise," she said.

Then she turned, grabbed Su Wan's hand, and they disappeared into the cargo bay.

The emergency pod launched a moment later, its trajectory set for the far edge of the debris field. The enemy sensors would pick it up—and hopefully, they would follow.

Lu Chen watched the pod vanish into the darkness.

"Now what?" Tanaka asked.

"Now we wait," he said. "And then we run."

### Chapter 36: 绝境中的光芒 (Light in Despair)

*"In the deepest darkness, even a single photon is a universe." — Su Wan's last recorded message, transmission unknown*

---

The waiting was the worst part.

Lu Chen sat in the silent shuttle, Tanaka beside him, both of them listening to the distant hum of the asteroid's geophysics. Hours passed. The enemy ships did not return. The debris field was quiet.

"Your friends might have escaped," Tanaka said.

"Or they might be dead."

"Both are possible. But we can't afford to assume the worst. We have to move."

Lu Chen activated the shuttle's engines, keeping them at minimal power. The craft lifted off the asteroid, drifting through the debris field, heading for the edge of the system where they could safely engage the FTL drive.

The journey to the Sable Nebula would take three weeks—their only stretch of time to think, to grieve, to plan.

When they finally emerged from the debris field, the starscape opened up. The white dwarf glowed in the distance, a reminder of the station and the dead who lay there. Lu Chen set the course, engaged the drive, and watched as the stars blurred into the hypnotic tunnel of faster-than-light travel.

"Three weeks," Tanaka said. "We should review the data from the crystal. All of it."

They did. Night after night, Lu Chen and Tanaka sat in the shuttle's small cabin, poring over Dr. Vance's logs, the Silent Ones' blueprints, the fragmented records of the Ether Drive's test.

The picture that emerged was terrifying.

The Underlayer was not a passive reservoir of energy. It was an ecosystem, teeming with patterns of quantum fluctuations that resembled—but were not—life. The Ether Drive's bridge was an intrusion, a wound in the fabric of reality that the ecosystem was trying to heal. But the attempted healing was violent. It warped space, bent time, created anomalies that could destabilize entire star systems.

"The Silent Ones locked the archive because they realized that the drive could not be used safely," Lu Chen said. "No matter how much tuning, no matter what frequency—the bridge would always cause damage."

Tanaka looked at him. "Then why did they leave the third node? Why preserve the tuning data at all?"

"Insurance. If a threat arose—something that could only be stopped by the Ether Drive—they wanted humanity to have the option. But they also wanted to make sure that option was difficult to reach."

"Difficult, but not impossible. Which means someone, somewhere, is going to use it. And when they do..."

"It will tear the universe apart."

They sat in silence. The shuttle hummed around them.

And then Hé spoke.

"Lu Chen. I have been analyzing the data from Su Wan's terminal—the fragment she saved before she left. She had been tracking something in the logs that I missed."

"What is it?"

"She found a transmission from the third node. A signal that was sent twenty-three years ago, from a ship designated 'ARK-1.' The ship's life support systems were failing. The descendants of the Silent Ones—the keepers of the tuning data—were dying. They broadcast a distress signal, requesting rescue. The signal was never answered."

"Are they still alive?"

"The signal has been silent for twenty-three years. But the ship's orbital patterns suggest it has been drifting, slowly losing altitude toward the nebula's core. If anyone survived, they would be running out of supplies."

"Then we're too late," Tanaka said.

"Not necessarily," Hé replied. "The ARK-1 was designed for generational survival. It has hydroponic bays, water reclamation, advanced medical facilities. A skeleton crew could have survived for decades if they rationed carefully. And there is one more detail: the third node's encryption key was updated six months ago."

Lu Chen sat up. "Updated? By whom?"

"Someone on the ARK-1. They are still alive. They are waiting."

Hope. A thin thread, but a thread nonetheless.

"Change course," Lu Chen said. "Maximum speed to the nebula."

"Already done," Tanaka replied, her hands on the controls. "We'll be there in nine days."

Nine days. It felt like an eternity. But it was also the closest they had ever been to the truth.

Lu Chen looked at the image of the ARK-1 on the display—a ship that had been lost for generations, carrying the last descendants of the Silent Ones. Somewhere inside, a person waited. A keeper of secrets. A survivor.

He touched the data crystal in his pocket, the cold metal pressing against his skin.

"We're coming," he whispered. "Hold on."

The stars blurred past, carrying them toward the nebula, toward the final piece of the puzzle.

And behind them, in the debris of a dead system, two red icons—Gao's cruiser and the remnants' vessel—were still searching, still hunting.

The race was not over.

It was just beginning.
